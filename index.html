Set this up once, then forget about it

Ten minutes on the sofa now saves you digging through menus in a field later. Everything here stays put until you deliberately change it.

  1. ISO AUTO Min. SS → Fast or Faster. The most useful setting on the camera, and nobody finds it by accident. It tells the camera to prefer a quick shutter over a low ISO. Quick shutter means sharp photo. This one setting prevents most blurry pictures before they happen.
  2. ISO Range Limit → 100 – 6400. Lets the camera brighten a dark scene by itself, but stops it going so far that photos turn grainy.
  3. Focus Mode → Continuous AF. The camera keeps adjusting focus for as long as you hold the button. Leave it here permanently — it's perfectly good for still subjects too, and it's required for the tracking modes.
  4. Subj Recog in AF → On, Recognition Target → Auto. The RX10 V has a dedicated chip for this. It recognises Human, Animal, Bird, Insect, Car/Train and Airplane, and will find and hold them on its own. Leave it on Auto for general use.
  5. Put Recognition Target on a custom button. Use Custom Key/Dial Set. to assign it to C1. Then you can flick from Auto to Bird in a second, without opening a menu — which is the difference between getting the shot and watching it fly off.
  6. Assign Cnt Sht Spd Bst w/ Hold to another custom button. Hold it mid-burst and the camera jumps to 30 frames a second. Lets you potter along at a sensible speed and go to full rate only for the two seconds that matter.
  7. The joystick (multi-selector) moves the focus box. New on this model and far quicker than poking the touchscreen. Nudge it to put the box on your subject.
  8. Learn the AF-ON button. Hold it and the camera focuses continuously without you touching the shutter. For anything that moves, this is how to work: hold AF-ON, follow the subject, press the shutter when it looks right.
  9. SteadyShot → On. Turn it off only on a tripod — Sony specifically warn against leaving it on there.
  10. File Format → JPEG, Image Quality → Fine or Extra Fine. Finished photos straight out of the camera, ready to send to anyone. You can add RAW later if you ever fancy editing them.
  11. Creative Look → ST. Natural colour. The out-of-camera colour and skin tones on this model are noticeably better than the old RX10 IV, so there's nothing to fix.
  12. Install Sony's Creators' App on your phone and pair it once. After that, photos come over by Wi-Fi with no cable and no card reader.

Three things about the body itself

  • The AF / MF switch on the body jumps straight to manual focus without a menu. You need it for the moon.
  • The focus range limiter switch has an ∞–3m position. Set it there for distant wildlife and the lens stops wasting time hunting towards close focus. It only takes effect past about 150mm, so you can leave it set and forget it.
  • The filter thread is 72mm. There's no ND filter built in, so if you ever want one — or a polariser for water and skies — that's the size to buy.

The one habit worth building

Zoom first, then focus. This camera can't hold focus while the lens is still travelling — it's the one genuine weakness of an otherwise excellent autofocus system. Frame roughly, let go of the zoom, then hold AF-ON and shoot.

Where everything is

The guide keeps naming buttons, so here they are. You only really need numbers 6, 7 and 14 to 16 — those are the ones that change how the camera focuses.

  1. Viewfinder. Put your eye to it and the screen switches over by itself. Steadier than holding the camera at arm's length — use it at long zoom.
  2. Diopter dial. Turn it until the viewfinder display looks sharp to your eyes. Do this once. If the viewfinder has ever looked slightly soft, this is why.
  3. MENU. Everything in the one-off setup list lives in here.
  4. Monitor. Touch-sensitive, and it tilts — pull it out to shoot low down or overhead.
  5. MOVIE. Starts and stops video, whatever else the camera is set to.
  6. AF-ON. Hold it to focus continuously without touching the shutter. The most useful button on the camera for anything that moves.
  7. Multi-selector. The little joystick. Moves the focus box around the frame.
  8. Fn. Brings up a grid of the settings you change most, without the full menu.
  9. Control wheel. Turns to change values; the edges press as four direction buttons.
  10. C3. A custom button you can assign anything to. In playback it's the delete button.
  11. Front lens ring. Can be set to zoom or to focus — see Lens Ring Setup.
  12. Aperture ring. Turns to set the f-number directly. Nice: no menu, no dial-hunting.
  13. Rear lens ring. The other configurable ring.
  14. Focus mode switch. AF or MF. Flick it to MF for the moon, or for a bird autofocus won't settle on.
  15. Focus hold button. Holds focus exactly where it is while you hold it down — useful when something keeps crossing in front of your subject.
  16. Focus range limiter. Set to ∞–3m for anything distant and the lens stops hunting through its close range.

Also worth knowing, on the top and the grip

  • The mode dial on top is where you pick A, S, M and the rest. AUTO sits on a small raised bump so you can find it without looking.
  • The Still / Movie / S&Q dial switches between photos, video and slow motion. Hold the little release catch on its front to turn it.
  • Control dials L and R next to it adjust whatever the current mode needs — shutter speed in S, for example.
  • C1 and C2 sit on top of the grip by the shutter button. These are the two to put Recognition Target and the burst speed boost on.
  • The W/T lever around the shutter button is the zoom.

Why. Indoor light is far dimmer than your eyes let you believe. Staying at the wide end keeps you at f/2.4, the brightest this lens ever gets, and that buys the fast shutter you need. 1/250 is the honest minimum for a moving toddler — at 1/60 you get a beautifully sharp sofa and a blurred child.

Small birds in the distance

This is the hardest focusing job the camera has, and it's worth saying plainly: it is genuinely difficult, and it is not you doing something wrong.

Sony's own manual lists what defeats their autofocus, and a small distant bird manages most of it at once — distant, low contrast, often in shade or back-lit against a bright sky, and frequently moving. Reviewers testing this model found the same thing: excellent on large or near subjects, occasionally beaten by small subjects far away. There's also a catch worth knowing — bird recognition struggles when the subject is already badly out of focus, so if the lens is hunting wildly it can't rescue itself. Your job is to get it roughly close, and then it locks on confidently.

The four settings that do the real work

These are the ones that specifically address distant birds, as opposed to general good practice. Three of them are buried, and none of them are on by default.

SettingSet it toWhat it actually does
AF Subj. Shift Sensitivity 1 (Locked on) Stops the camera abandoning your bird for a nearer branch. On 5 it hops to whatever is most eye-catching; on 1 it stubbornly stays put. This is the single most useful change for birds in a hedge or a tree.
Priority Set in AF-C AF Refuses to fire unless focus is actually achieved. The default lets the shutter go regardless — and at this distance you cannot tell from the screen whether it got there. Better to miss the frame than fill the card with near-misses.
Focus range limiter ∞–3m A physical switch on the body. The lens stops wasting time searching the close end of its range, which is most of the hunting you're hearing. Active past about 150mm, so just leave it set.
Zoom Range Optical zoom only Keeps you off Clear Image Zoom and Digital Zoom. Both make the bird look bigger on screen while giving the focus system less real detail to work with — and digital zoom disables some of the focus tools below. Shoot at 600mm and crop afterwards instead.

The sequence that works

Not a settings change, a habit. In this order, every time:

  1. Find the bird with your eyes first, not through the camera. Note what it's next to — the dead branch, the third fence post. At 600mm the view is so narrow that hunting for a sparrow through the viewfinder is hopeless.
  2. Raise the camera already zoomed out, then zoom in. Get the bird in the frame at 200mm where it's easy, then zoom to 600mm keeping it centred. Trying to find it at full zoom from cold is how you lose it.
  3. Let go of the zoom before you focus. The camera can't hold focus while the lens is still travelling. Zoom, stop, then focus.
  4. Put the box on the bird with the joystick, then hold AF-ON. Don't ask the camera to find the bird in the whole frame — tell it where to look. Expand Spot is forgiving if your aim wobbles slightly.
  5. Wait for the frame to settle on the bird before you shoot. With bird recognition on, the camera draws a box when it has found one. That box appearing is your green light. If it never appears, go to the manual fallback below rather than firing hopefully.
  6. Take three or four, not thirty. A perched bird doesn't need 30 frames a second, and small bursts make checking focus afterwards far less miserable.

Where to put the focus box

Autofocus works by finding edges, so aim at one. The middle of a uniformly brown body gives it nothing to grip. Go for the boundary where the bird meets the sky, or the dark cap against a pale cheek, or the eye and beak — anywhere light meets dark.

For the same reason, a bird on a bare branch against open sky is enormously easier than the same bird inside a bush. If you have the choice, wait for it to hop somewhere cleaner.

When autofocus simply won't

Some birds in some light will defeat it. Don't fight it for ten minutes — switch to manual, which on a static perched bird is often quicker anyway:

  • Flick the body switch to MF.
  • Turn on Peaking Display, with Peaking Level High and Peaking Colour Red. Red outlines appear over whatever is sharp — turn the ring until they land on the bird.
  • Use Focus Magnifier to zoom the display in and confirm it properly. Half-pressing the shutter cancels the magnified view when you're ready to shoot.
  • Or use DMF: the camera autofocuses, and then you fine-tune by hand without leaving the mode. Often the best of both.

The uncomfortable bit about distance

No setting fixes being too far away. A sparrow at 40 metres is a small sparrow even at 600mm, and once the bird covers only a handful of pixels there isn't enough detail for the camera to recognise or for you to enjoy.

Closing the distance beats every setting on this page. Patience, a hide, a parked car as a blind, a feeder positioned where you can sit near it. Failing that, remember you have 20 megapixels — framing a bit loose at 600mm and cropping later often gives a better result than straining for reach you don't have.

Based on a 15cm bird and the lens at 600mm, where the camera's view is roughly six centimetres wide for every metre of distance. Halve the distance and you double the bird — which is why walking twenty metres closer beats any menu setting.

The one bit of theory: you have two shutters

This trips people up, and it's the reason a recipe can look right and still not work. Shutter Type in the menu has two settings and they are genuinely different cameras.

If the photo came out wrong

Blurry, and I was zoomed right in
Shutter speed was too slow. Switch to S and set 1/1000. This is the cause about nine times in ten.
The person is a dark shape against a bright sky
The camera exposed for the sky, not the face. Turn Exposure Comp. to +1. There's no flash built into this camera, so the other answer is to move them — turn them so the light is on their face rather than behind them.
Grainy and speckled
ISO climbed high because the light was poor. Zoom back towards 24mm to get f/2.4, or move closer to a window or a lamp.
Nothing looks properly sharp anywhere
You were probably at f/11 or f/16. Go back to f/5.6.
Faint bands or stripes across an indoor photo
Electronic shutter reacting to LED or fluorescent lighting. Set Shutter Type → Mechanical Shutter.
The camera won't let me pick 30 frames a second
You're on the mechanical shutter, which doesn't offer Hi+ or Hi. Set Shutter Type → Electronic Shutter first.
Focus hunted while I was zooming
Known limitation, not a fault. Zoom, let go, then hold AF-ON and shoot.
The sky is white and featureless
Overexposed. Exposure Comp. to −0.7 and the clouds come back.
Focus landed on the branch, not the bird
